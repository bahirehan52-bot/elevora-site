What should a small business prepare before building a website?

Published 19 November 2026 · By Elevora

A business website is easier to build when its owner can explain what the business sells, who it serves and what a visitor should do next. You do not need a perfect brand book or professionally written copy before starting. You do need accurate information. Collecting it first reduces guesswork, prevents missing pages and makes it easier to judge whether a proposed design actually serves the business.

1. Write down the main business facts

Prepare the business name, service area, opening hours, phone number, email address and the best way for customers to contact you. If customers visit a physical location, include the correct address and directions. If you serve customers remotely, explain which locations you can realistically serve. Check every phone number and link before the website goes live.

2. Describe the offer in plain language

For each product or service, write down what it includes, who it is for, what problem it solves and any limits a customer should know about. Avoid vague claims such as “best quality” unless you can explain what that means. A useful service description answers practical questions: what will the customer receive, how does the process work, how long does it usually take, and what information is needed to request a quote?

3. Collect real photos and proof

Use photographs of your actual shop, team, products, completed work or premises when you have permission to publish them. Make sure images are clear and relevant. If you are a new business without customer reviews, do not invent testimonials, client logos or project results. You can explain your process, show clearly labelled sample concepts, and add genuine customer feedback later with permission.

4. Decide which pages visitors need

  • Home: a short explanation of the business, its main offer and the next action.
  • Services or products: specific descriptions, inclusions and useful details.
  • About: who operates the business, its location and relevant experience.
  • Contact: working phone, email, WhatsApp, address or enquiry form.
  • Policies: privacy, returns, delivery or other policies that genuinely apply to the business.

A small business does not need dozens of thin pages. Start with the pages that answer real customer questions. Add a separate page only when it has enough distinct information to be useful.

5. Define one primary action

Choose the most useful next step for your business: request a quote, call, book an appointment, visit the shop or buy a product. Make that action easy to find. Secondary actions can exist, but a page with many competing buttons makes it harder for visitors to decide what to do.

Before development: put your business facts, service descriptions, real images and contact details in one folder. Ask someone unfamiliar with the business to read the content and tell you what is still unclear.

Final checklist: accurate business details, clear offer, original images or properly licensed assets, real proof only, useful pages and one obvious contact action. If an item is not ready, mark it as missing rather than filling the gap with invented information.

How to structure a homepage for a local service business

Published 19 November 2026 · By Elevora

A homepage should help a first-time visitor answer three questions quickly: what does this business do, can it help someone like me, and how do I contact it? A visually attractive page can still fail if visitors have to search for those answers. The sections below provide a practical order. Not every business needs every section, but each included section should have a clear purpose.

Start with a specific headline

Use a headline that names the service and, where useful, the location or customer type. “Plumbing repairs in Faisalabad” tells a visitor more than “Solutions for a better tomorrow.” Follow it with one or two sentences explaining the work you handle. Put the main action beside this introduction, such as “Call for a quote” or “Message us on WhatsApp.”

Show the main services

List the services customers actually ask for. Give each one a short description that explains what is included. If the business offers many services, group them into sensible categories and link to a detailed page. Do not list services the business cannot currently provide just to make the website look larger.

Add evidence that can be checked

Useful evidence may include real project photographs, a physical location, relevant qualifications, an explanation of the work process or genuine reviews. Give context to project images: describe the task and what was completed. Do not publish private customer details or use a review without permission. If the business has no case studies yet, explain the service process instead of fabricating a portfolio.

Explain what happens after contact

Visitors are more likely to enquire when they know what to expect. Describe the steps accurately. For example: send the project details, receive a response, discuss the scope, then receive a quote. Do not promise response times or delivery dates unless the business can consistently meet them.

Answer common questions

Choose questions customers genuinely ask before purchasing: service area, availability, payment methods, what is included, how quotations work and what preparation is required. Give direct answers. If prices vary, explain which factors affect the quote rather than displaying a misleading fixed price.

Finish with contact details that work

Repeat the main action near the end of the page. Make phone numbers tap-to-call on mobile, use a valid email link, and test the WhatsApp link. If the business has a public address, show it accurately. Keep the contact page consistent with the details in the footer and business profiles.

Simple homepage order: headline and contact action → services → genuine proof → how the process works → frequently asked questions → contact details.

After publishing, test the homepage on a phone. Check whether the headline is visible without excessive scrolling, buttons are easy to tap, text is readable, and images do not make the page slow. Ask a person who has not seen the site before to find a service and contact the business. Their difficulty points to what needs improving.

How to make WhatsApp enquiries easier from a business website

Published 19 November 2026 · By Elevora

For many small businesses, WhatsApp is a familiar way to answer questions and discuss orders. A website can link directly to a business chat, but the link should be easy to use and should not create confusion about who is receiving the message. The goal is to help a visitor start a useful conversation with the least effort.

Use the correct business number

Use the full international phone number in the WhatsApp click-to-chat link, without spaces, brackets or the plus sign. For a Pakistan number, the country code is 92 and the leading zero of the local mobile number is removed. Confirm the number is registered with WhatsApp and controlled by the business before publishing it.

Prefill a helpful message

A prefilled message can tell the customer what details to include. For example: “Hello, I found your website and would like to ask about [service/product]. My location is [area].” Keep it short and let the visitor edit it before sending. Do not put sensitive personal details into a prefilled URL.

Place the link where it answers a question

  • Put a primary WhatsApp button near the main service or product introduction.
  • Repeat it beside relevant service details when a visitor may have a specific question.
  • Include it on the contact page with the business hours or expected response window, if known.
  • Use a consistent label such as “Chat on WhatsApp” so visitors know what will happen.

Do not make WhatsApp the only contact route

Some visitors cannot or do not want to use WhatsApp. Provide another suitable option, such as a phone number or email address. If you use a contact form, explain where the message goes and whether the site stores it. Do not claim that a form is private or secure beyond what the actual setup supports.

Test the experience on a phone

Open the website on a mobile device and tap every WhatsApp button. Check that the correct business account opens, the prefilled text is readable, and the message is not sent automatically. The visitor should always review and send the message themselves. Also test the link on a device where WhatsApp is not installed; provide a phone or email alternative for that situation.

Set realistic expectations

If the business replies only during certain hours, state those hours. Avoid claims such as “instant reply” unless someone is actually available to respond. A clear statement such as “Messages are answered during business hours” is more useful than a promise the business cannot keep.

Example: a customer-facing button can open a chat with a short draft message asking about a service. The visitor checks the message, adds their details and chooses whether to send it.

Finally, review the number and link whenever the business changes its contact details. A broken chat button is worse than no button because it sends a ready-to-contact customer to a dead end.
